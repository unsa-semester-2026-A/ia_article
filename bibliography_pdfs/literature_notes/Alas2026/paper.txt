ABSTRACT Real-time Multi-Camera Vehicle Tracking (MCVT) systems are challenging to design and
deploy due to the need for real-time processing at the edge, as well as issues such as high noise, low
image resolution, and reidentification. With the significant advances of computer vision techniques and
transformer-based models for image classification, new opportunities have emerged for MCVT. Moreover,
the growth of urban populations and the increasing demand for sustainable mobility have intensified
the need for scalable traffic monitoring solutions. Despite recent progress, several critical challenges
remain unresolved, including unreliable re-identification under severe occlusions and viewpoint changes,
limited temporal synchronisation between heterogeneous cameras, and the lack of architectures that operate
efficiently on fog and edge infrastructures. In this paper, we propose a scalable, low-latency MCVT
pipeline designed for heterogeneous CCTV networks deployed across the edge–fog continuum. The
system integrates lightweight vehicle detection, transformer-based appearance embeddings, and topologyand travel-time-constrained cross-camera association within a modular deployment-aware architecture.
Experimental validation on both public benchmarks and a real-world logistics hub demonstrates stable
identity preservation, correctly identifying more than 80% of vehicle matches under deployment conditions
while maintaining strict computational constraints. The system sustains real-time performance (20–29 FPS
per stream) under CPU-based detection across 27 CCTV cameras and seven edge nodes, demonstrating
scalable multi-camera tracking under production-level constraints.
INDEX TERMS Fog computing, multi-camera tracking, traffic monitoring, vehicle reidentification.
I. INTRODUCTION

Real-time multi-camera vehicle tracking (MCVT) in urban
environments is a priority capability for smart city traffic
management and large logistics hubs. It involves continuously detecting and following vehicles across a network
of street camera feeds to understand traffic flow, monitor
congestion, and improve safety. Unlike single-camera tracking, the multi-camera setting poses additional challenges:
each camera may have a different viewpoint and resolution,
video streams often suffer from noise (e.g., low light or
weather effects), frequent occlusions occur in dense traffic,
and associating vehicle identities across non-overlapping
cameras is notoriously tricky. In a city-scale deployment,

in research settings may struggle in edge environments due to

Recent advances in deep learning have indeed dramatically
improved visual tracking accuracy. In single-camera MultiObject Tracking (MOT), methods such as DeepSORT [1]
combine a fast Kalman filter tracker with deep neural
network appearance embedding to associate detections over
time. Transformer-based trackers have taken this further:
for instance, TrackFormer [2], TransTrack [3], and AttentionTrack [4] introduce transformer attention to detect and
track multiple objects in video frames jointly. End-to-end
transformer models, such as the Multiple-Object Tracking
Transformer (MOTR) [5], treat object tracking as a direct
set prediction problem, propagating learned track queries
frame by frame. However, they come at a cost: the networks
are large and require GPU acceleration to run in realtime. Deploying a MOTR-style model on dozens of camera
feeds would likely overwhelm typical edge computing nodes.
There is a growing interest in lightweight computer vision
techniques that trade some accuracy for efficiency. For
example, in static-camera scenarios, background subtraction
and motion segmentation can serve as simpler vehicle
detectors. BYTETrack [6] has demonstrated that improved
data association strategies can substantially improve accuracy
without altering the underlying detector, using low-score
detection boxes to link previous ones. These approaches
achieve strong results on academic benchmarks, particularly
in handling occlusions and maintaining identities over
extended video sequences.
In the domain of multi-camera tracking, most existing
solutions follow a decoupled two-step approach: first,
perform independent tracking on each camera, then link
the tracklets belonging to the same object across cameras
using appearance features or spatio-temporal reasoning. For
example, the widely used CityFlow benchmark [7] defines
a city-scale multi-camera vehicle tracking task where there
is a detection and single-camera tracking system for each
video, and a separate association algorithm matches vehicles
between cameras. Appearance-based vehicle reidentification
(ReID) is a key component in many methods. Deep learning
ReID models are trained on datasets like VeRi-776 [8] or
VRIC [9] to encode each vehicle’s visual features into an
embedding vector, ensuring that the exact vehicle produces
similar embeddings across different cameras. License plate
recognition and spatio-temporal constraints are often incorporated to improve robustness. For instance, if an OCR
module can read a vehicle’s license plate, it provides a nearly
unique ID to corroborate the visual match, a technique used in

intelligent transport systems for decades. Reference [10] has
even explored using vehicle trajectory prediction to aid crosscamera association, effectively estimating where and when a
vehicle might reappear, to narrow down candidate matches.
The proposed approach is motivated by the gap between
bleeding-edge accuracy and practical application in constrained environments. The system is a modular, scalable,
and efficient MCVT system tailored for fog/edge computing.
Instead of a monolithic system, the proposal is split into
specialised modules: (1) a vehicle detection module using
traditional computer vision techniques to identify vehicles in
each camera frame quickly; 2) a feature extraction module
that computes a compact appearance descriptor for each
vehicle; and (3) a multi-source data association module that
combines the appearance descriptors and spatio-temporal
context to reidentify vehicles across cameras and reconstruct
complete trajectories. By splitting the problem into these
stages, each stage can be optimised or scaled independently.
This modular design is inherently scalable, allowing more
processing nodes to be added to handle additional camera
streams or higher frame rates. The system can also degrade
gracefully by skipping or simplifying specific modules if
resources are constrained.
This work addresses real-time multi-camera vehicle
tracking by proposing a deployment-aware architecture
that integrates motion consistency, appearance learning,
and physical plausibility constraints within an edge–fog
computing framework. The contributions are threefold.
First, we introduce a lightweight direction-aware centroid
linking strategy for single-camera tracking that enforces
multi-scale motion consistency without relying on deep tracking backbones. The proposed association logic combines
spatial overlap gating with short-, medium-, and long-term
directional constraints, enabling stable tracklet formation
under edge computing limitations. Second, we design a
topology- and travel-time-constrained cross-camera association framework that couples compact transformer-based
appearance embeddings with physically grounded feasibility
checks. This formulation reduces implausible cross-camera
identity merges in non-overlapping CCTV networks. Third,
we validate the complete system in a real-world deployment
spanning 27 CCTV cameras and seven edge nodes, sustaining 20–29 FPS per stream under CPU-based detection.
The results demonstrate scalable, low-latency multi-camera
tracking under production-level constraints.

MOT is traditionally formulated as a tracking-by-detection
problem: objects are first detected in each frame, and then
these detections are linked over time to form trajectories.
Early approaches relied on simple appearance cues and
motion models (e.g., Kalman filters) to associate detections,
as exemplified by SORT [11], a lightweight tracker that
uses bounding box overlap and constant-velocity motion
for data association. An improvement over SORT was
DeepSORT [1], which adds a deep CNN to compute
appearance embeddings for each detected object, making the
association more robust to occlusions and crossings. In recent
years, transformer-based trackers have redefined the MOT
landscape. These methods integrate detection and tracking
into a unified architecture, using attention mechanisms to
model object interactions over space and time. For example,
TrackFormer [2] treats object tracks as learnable queries and
carries them through a transformer decoder from frame to
frame. TransTrack [3] similarly introduces a transformer that
takes both previous frame tracks and current frame detections
as input to predict new positions. MOTR [5] and its improved
version MOTRv2 [12] eliminate the separate detection stage
(they use a set of track queries in a DETR-like transformer
to simultaneously detect new objects and update existing
tracks in each frame). Although these end-to-end approaches
demonstrate excellent performance on benchmarks, their
high complexity requires powerful hardware. Even these
advanced trackers struggle with prolonged occlusions or
crowded scenes when limited to one camera view [13].
Lightweight association strategies, such as ByteTrack, OCSORT, and GIAOTracker, improve MOT by strengthening
motion-centric matching and fragmentation repair without
requiring costly deep components. ByteTrack employs a
dual-threshold policy that leverages low-score detections
only to continue existing tracks. OC-SORT introduces
observation-centric re-updates and a direction-aware cost
to handle occlusions and non-linear motion. GIAOTracker,
on the other hand, combines online association, a global link
stage, and post-processing informed by camera/object motion
to reconnect broken tracklets.

Another line of work in multicamera tracking leverages additional contextual cues. One example is trajectory
prediction for association, which uses a future trajectory
predictor to estimate where a person (in their case) might
reappear and integrate that into the matching metric for crosscamera tracking. This is particularly helpful in large camera
networks, where a target might temporarily disappear; a
motion model can narrow the search space of possible
reidentities [16]. In traffic surveillance, an analogous idea
is to use the topology of the road network. Reference [17]
presents a system that executes deep learning detection
and tracking algorithms directly on camera nodes and
communicates vehicle metadata via low-power short-range
radio, enabling decentralised, privacy-preserving tracking
suitable for parking lots and intersections. However, it does
not utilise appearance-based ReID, which makes identity
association weak when cameras are spatially disjoint or the
angles differ significantly from deployment to deployment.
License Plate Recognition (LPR) is another powerful cue
in vehicle tracking that has been used in practical systems:
reading the plate gives a direct ID, which can significantly
simplify multicamera matching (vehicles with the same plate
are considered identical). However, LPR is not infallible
(plates can be obscured or unreadable in motion), and it must
be combined with visual matching for cases where the plate
is missing or duplicated.
Bird’s Eye View (BEV) and V2X-based Tracking: An
emerging trend, especially in autonomous driving research,
is to combine multiple camera views into a unified bird’s-eye
view representation for detection and tracking. For example,
BEVFormer [18] and PETR [19] employ transformers to
aggregate features from multiple cameras and time steps
to detect all objects in a global BEV coordinate frame.
This approach can naturally handle multicamera tracking
in the sense that, once objects are located in BEV with
a unique identifier, switching cameras is just observing
the same BEV track from different angles. However, the

C. ReIdentification MODELS

Reidentification (ReID) aims to match the same object
across different cameras or viewpoints by learning robust
embeddings that preserve identity despite variations in angle,
illumination, and other factors. Recent progress [20] has
focused on the adoption of Vision Transformers (ViT) [21].
In particular, TransReID [22] achieved state-of-the-art results
on vehicle datasets such as VeRi-776 and VehicleID [23] by
enhancing a ViT-base backbone with ReID-specific modules.
A key characteristic of modern ReID systems is the use of
multi-stage training strategies to maximise feature discrimination and improve cosine similarity between embeddings
of the same object. Typically, this involves three steps:
(1) initialising with a robust pre-trained backbone (e.g., ViT
trained on ImageNet [24] or large-scale self-supervised data),
(2) fine-tuning on an intermediate supervised dataset related
to the target domain, and (3) performing final fine-tuning on
the target ReID dataset with metric-learning objectives such
as triplet or contrastive loss.
Several works illustrate the benefits of this progressive
approach. For example, [25] introduced a multi-domain
learning pipeline in which a ResNet-based model was
first trained on a mixture of real and synthetic vehicle
images, then fine-tuned on real-world data while freezing
lower layers. Similarly, [26] demonstrated that incorporating diverse synthetic vehicles during training yields more
discriminative embeddings and stronger generalisation in
real-world deployment scenarios. VehicleNet [27] further
exemplifies this principle by applying a two-stage progressive
scheme: first training with a classification loss on a large
mixed-source dataset to learn generic representations, then
fine-tuning on the target domain to refine embeddings. Within
the ViT paradigm, self-supervised pretraining has also shown
substantial benefits. For instance, [28] proposed pretraining
a ViT model on the large-scale unlabeled dataset using selfsupervised objectives, followed by supervised fine-tuning on
ReID datasets. This multi-stage strategy effectively narrows
the domain gap and improves downstream embedding
performance. More recently, [29] leveraged a pretrained
ViT and applied multi-aspect-ratio fine-tuning with feature
fusion, showing that specialised intermediate training can
enhance robustness in vehicle ReID tasks. These results
highlight the importance of staged fine-tuning and domain
adaptation in achieving robust ReID performance.

works have focused on strengthening discriminative cues
and evaluation protocols for vehicle ReID under challenging

B. VEHICLE DETECTION PIPELINE

The system reduces bandwidth and latency by executing
initial vehicle detection on fog devices using traditional
lightweight computer vision methods. These procedures
operate efficiently without requiring GPUs, making the
system suitable for edge processing environments. The choice
of traditional approaches is motivated by several practical
considerations: (1) they demand very low computational
resources, which makes them well-suited for edge devices
with limited hardware capacity or legacy CCTV cameras;
(2) they are straightforward to tune without large-scale
training, simplifying deployment and adaptation to new
camera setups; (3) background subtraction enables low-cost
extraction of regions of interest (ROIs) before applying more
expensive detection stages, thereby reducing computation
when most of the scene remains static; and (4) they optimize
memory usage, since only cropped candidate regions need
to be stored or processed, minimizing RAM consumption
and I/O overhead when disk-based storage is required. The
detection pipeline consists of four stages: (1) bounding box
identification, (2) postprocessing, (3) real-time tracking, and
(4) direction identification.

2) POST PROCESSING

Once the algorithm identifies the contours, they are passed
to bounding rectangles to determine if there are contours that
can be grouped. This step is necessary because background
subtraction frequently produces fragmented blobs for a single
physical vehicle, especially in low-resolution CCTV or
under weak texture conditions. For example, large trucks
or vans with uniform surfaces (e.g., white trailers) may
generate motion response mainly on their borders or shadow
edges, while interior regions remain partially absorbed by
the background model. As a result, a single vehicle can
appear as multiple small bounding boxes that should not be
treated independently. If these fragments are not merged,
they can lead to duplicated track initializations, unstable
crop geometry, and increased identity fragmentation in
downstream tracking and reidentification stages.
The postprocessing stage examines those bounding rectangles that meet a specific percentage of intersection, grouping
spatially consistent fragments into a single candidate vehicle
region. Since grouping should be transitive (i.e., if contour
A overlaps with B, and B overlaps with C, all three should
belong to the same vehicle), the system applies depth-first
search (DFS) as a clustering technique, representing contours
as nodes and intersections between those contours as edges.
Finally, the graph is divided into the different connected
components explored, with each one of them forming a
subgraph that encompasses the potential vehicle bounding
box.

D. TRAJECTORY RECONSTRUCTION

This module, responsible for reconstructing vehicle trajectories across different camera views, uses the embeddings and
metadata (camera ID, timestamp, and directions) produced
in the previous stages to establish spatio-temporal links
between detections. The process begins by matching vehicle
detections from different cameras that are part of the
same predefined zone. These matches are called zone
events. In cases where cameras have overlapping views,
the same vehicle may be detected simultaneously by two
cameras. The system identifies these duplicate instances
VOLUME 14, 2026

using a combination of high embedding cosine similarity and
near-synchronised timing. Such duplicates are resolved by
consolidating them into a single track so that the vehicle is
counted only once. In case of ambiguous directions, both
directions are discarded and the direction of the new event is
set to unknown. If no match is found between two cameras
in the same zone within a specified temporal range, the
unmatched events are still stored for further analysis, but with
lower priority. Vehicles in zones with only one camera are
always stored as zone events.
Once candidate matches are stored, they serve as the
foundation for re-identification across zones and the reconstruction of complete trajectories. A link between zone events
is called a street event, and a trajectory is defined as a set
of linked street events. Mercabarna’s camera layout provides
natural constraints: cameras cover partially overlapping or
sequential zones such that a vehicle travelling through
the hub will appear in a predictable order. These spatial
relationships are encoded as prior knowledge. Thus, when
a new detection occurs, the search for a match is restricted
to a pool of candidate embeddings from cameras that
could logically observe the same vehicle next. Additionally,
temporal constraints are imposed based on the estimated
travel time between zones, which reduces false match
attempts by pruning physically implausible pairings.
To further control false associations, we use two cosine
similarity thresholds depending on the reliability of the
motion metadata. When the street directions are known and
consistent, directionality acts as an additional discriminative
cue that reduces the effective ambiguity of the candidate
set. Therefore, a more permissive appearance threshold is
sufficient (e.g., τdir = 0.65), allowing matches under
challenging viewpoint/illumination changes while remaining
constrained by direction feasibility. In contrast, when direction is unknown (e.g., due to reversing, stops, or conflicting
motion estimates), the system must rely almost exclusively
on appearance similarity. In this regime, we adopt a stricter
threshold (e.g., τunk = 0.85) to compensate for the
increased uncertainty and to avoid visually plausible but
incorrect cross-camera merges, which are common under
low-resolution CCTV and repetitive vehicle fleets.

D. ABLATION STUDY

The previous subsections evaluated the main components
of the pipeline in isolation and then reported end-toend multi-camera re-identification performance under real
operational conditions in Mercabarna. While these results
validate the overall feasibility of the proposed modular
architecture, they do not isolate the contribution of key design
choices that affect identity preservation across cameras.
To this end, we conduct a targeted ablation study focusing
on three high-impact components: (i) the single-camera
association mechanism responsible for forming temporally
stable tracklets that later feed the cross-camera linking stage,
(ii) the appearance embedding model used for cross-camera
association, and (iii) the YOLO refinement stage that filters
and disambiguates detections prior to embedding extraction.
All ablations are evaluated on the same Mercabarna
multi-camera protocol described in Section IV, using the
same manually annotated set of 226 ground-truth vehicle
matches. We report Identity Precision (IDP), Identity Recall
(IDR), IDF1, and Uniqueness. Unless explicitly stated,
all hyperparameters, camera topology constraints, crosscamera similarity thresholds, and aggregation strategy (tracklevel average embedding) are held constant to ensure that
performance differences can be attributed to the ablated
component only.

slightly increase recall, as more detections are forwarded to

but a major contributor to association correctness. When
spatial ordering constraints (restricting candidate matches to
logically reachable cameras/zones) and temporal feasibility
constraints (travel-time windows) are removed, the matching
problem degenerates into a global appearance-only retrieval
over a substantially larger candidate pool. This drastically
increases the number of plausible-but-incorrect pairings
in Mercabarna, where many vehicles share similar visual

trucks). As a result, identity precision collapses and the
overall identity consistency degrades, indicating that false
cross-camera merges become the dominant failure mode.
Interestingly, Uniqueness decreases less sharply than in the
weakest tracking ablation, suggesting that the primary impact
of removing spatio-temporal constraints is not increased
fragmentation, but rather incorrect linking across physically
implausible camera transitions. Finally, the recall decrease
is consistent with error propagation: early false matches
‘‘consume’’ identities and prevent the correct downstream
links from being established, turning what could have been
recoverable associations into missed links.
Overall, the ablations indicate that the appearance embedding is the dominant factor shaping multi-camera association quality (IDP/IDR/IDF1), while single-camera tracking
mainly affects downstream identity continuity by modulating
fragmentation (Uniqueness). The YOLO refinement stage
primarily acts as an upstream noise filter that enforces a
clear precision–recall trade-off by suppressing false positives
and ambiguous crops. Finally, the trajectory reconstruction
priors (camera adjacency and travel-time feasibility) prove
essential to prevent physically implausible matches, thereby
preserving high precision in real deployments, particularly
under low-resolution CCTV and visually repetitive vehicle
fleets.
E. DEPLOYMENT TEST

We implemented the proposed system in Mercabarna
(Barcelona), a major hub of the wholesale market with
significant daily truck and vehicle traffic. The deployment
consists of twenty-seven CCTV cameras covering key
entry/exit points and internal road intersections of the
facility, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Seven edge processing units
(GEEKOM Mini PC XT12 Pro, Intel i7-12650H, 32 GB

to different cameras depending on stream resolution and
activity. The system operates in real time, with edge devices
processing each video stream at approximately 20–29 FPS
depending on scene activity (high activity corresponds to
6–8 vehicles simultaneously within the ROI). The Mercabarna environment introduces challenging real-world conditions, including strong illumination variation across the day,
weather changes, dense traffic, and frequent occlusions due
to similar-looking forklifts and delivery vehicles as well as
pedestrians crossing the road.

This work presented a modular, edge–fog multi-camera vehicle tracking system that decouples detection, feature embedding, and multi-source association to meet real-time constraints under limited compute. Deployed across 27 CCTV
cameras with seven edge units sustaining 20–29 FPS, the
architecture reduces bandwidth, keeps latency low, and scales
by adding nodes per need. In this context, the system
provides a practical foundation for deploying city-scale
vehicle tracking systems that are efficient, accurate, and
resilient, while remaining open to integration with the next
generation of detection and association algorithms.
The implementation deliberately prioritises efficiency
and operational robustness on resource-constrained edge
hardware. However, this design choice introduces a structural
limitation. The reliance on traditional computer-vision-based
detection and a highly tuned real-time tracking component
makes the system closely adapted to the deployment scenario.
While this improves stability and throughput, transferring the
pipeline to substantially different environments (e.g., altered
viewpoints, traffic density, or scene dynamics) may require
non-trivial retuning.
From a performance perspective, the ablation analysis
revealed a clear component–metric relationship. Appearance
representation is the primary driver of multi-camera association quality, directly impacting precision in cross-camera
matching. In contrast, the tracking module largely governs
identity fragmentation, reflected in the Uniqueness metric.
Finally, the detection stage determines the precision–recall
balance by controlling false positives and bounding-box
consistency. These findings provide a principled roadmap for
future improvements.
Building on this foundation, several extensions offer
a clear path to improved accuracy and robustness without sacrificing real-time performance. First, future work
will focus on migrating the detection stage toward compact, quantized deep neural models. Replacing the current
background-subtraction-based approach with lightweight
quantized detectors would increase robustness to dynamic
urban scenes while preserving real-time performance on
the same edge hardware. More stable detections are
expected to reduce downstream track fragmentation and
improve both precision and Uniqueness without compromising throughput. Second, improving trajectory detection and cross-camera vehicle linking represents a central

research direction. The ablation results demonstrate that
spatial ordering constraints and travel-time feasibility windows are not merely efficiency heuristics but fundamental
mechanisms for association correctness. Future extensions
will therefore explore more expressive trajectory models
that integrate topology priors, dynamic travel-time estimation, and probabilistic gating within a unified formulation. Finally, the re-identification head can be reinforced
through more expressive metric-learning objectives and
training strategies tailored to viewpoint variation and occlusion. Since appearance representation was identified as
the principal driver of multi-camera association quality,
improving embedding discriminability through stronger
contrastive formulations and domain-adaptive augmentation
is expected to yield measurable gains in cross-camera
precision.
Overall, the proposed system establishes a practical
real-time baseline for edge-deployed MCVT. Its modular
architecture enables targeted upgrades at the detector, tracker,
or embedding level without architectural redesign. This
preserves the efficiency principles of the edge–fog paradigm
while providing a controlled and technically grounded path
toward higher precision, stronger identity stability, and
improved cross-domain transferability.
