\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Real-Time Vehicle Detection and Tracking Baselines}

Real-time vehicle detection from video has been a foundational problem in intelligent transport systems,
and a growing body of work has converged on the YOLO family of detectors as the de~facto backbone for
this task. Gomaa et al.~\cite{Gomaa2022} attack the problem of high computational cost in vehicle
counting on fixed cameras. Their goal is to propose a fast counting strategy that avoids the overhead
of complex multi-object trackers. To this end, they combine a YOLOv2 detector with motion
feature-point analysis via K-means clustering and a Kanade--Lucas--Tomasi (KLT) optical-flow tracker.
The result is a throughput of 18.7 FPS with improved counting accuracy over prior methods.
The main contribution is demonstrating that lightweight point tracking, rather than a full-scale
re-identification pipeline, is sufficient for accurate real-time counting on static scenes.
The primary limitation, however, is that the method relies on axis-aligned bounding boxes and provides
no mechanism for handling class imbalance or discriminating between moving and stationary vehicles.

A more recent and widely cited baseline is the work of Kumar et al.~\cite{Kumar2023}, who
address the failure of earlier systems under varying vehicle scales, occlusions, and diverse
road conditions. Their objective is an effective real-time detection and tracking scheme for
highway traffic management. Methodologically, they fuse a YOLOv5 detector (operating at
140 FPS) with the DeepSORT tracker, supported by a Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm
for identity assignment. On the BDD100K and PASCAL benchmarks, the pipeline achieves
91.25\% precision, 93.52\% recall, and 92.18\% mAP. The contribution establishes a robust
and reproducible reference for the YOLO + DeepSORT paradigm that subsequent works
routinely cite as a performance floor. As with Gomaa et al., however, the method uses
horizontal bounding boxes and neither addresses class imbalance nor the discrimination
between moving and parked vehicles.

Bin Zuraimi and Kamaru Zaman~\cite{BinZuraimi2021} evaluate model latency and speed-accuracy trade-offs for real-time surveillance. Using a mid-range GPU, they compare YOLOv3, YOLOv3\allowbreak-tiny, YOLOv4, and YOLOv4\allowbreak-tiny (with Darknet weights) paired with the DeepSORT algorithm for tracking. On their custom traffic dataset, YOLOv4 achieves the highest mAP@0.5 of 82.08\% at 14.12~FPS, while YOLOv4-tiny yields 76.14\% at 40.11~FPS on a single GTX~1660~Ti. This empirically confirms that lightweight YOLO variants can sustain genuine real-time throughput on consumer-grade GPUs without catastrophic accuracy loss. Critically, all tested configurations use standard axis-aligned bounding boxes, leaving vehicle orientation unaddressed, which is a necessary capability in intersection-level traffic analysis.

\subsection{Scalable Deployment on Low-Cost Hardware}

Beyond algorithmic accuracy, practical traffic monitoring must run on embedded and
edge-computing infrastructure that municipalities can realistically afford. Balamuralidhar
et al.~\cite{Balamuralidhar2021} tackle the challenge of running a joint detection,
tracking, and speed-estimation system in real time on a UAV payload constrained to an
Nvidia Jetson Xavier~NX. Their multi-task architecture attaches a segmentation head to a
shared backbone, enabling simultaneous detection and scene understanding. Compared to the
prior state of the art, the system achieves a 4.8\% improvement in mAP while improving
processing speed by 91.4\%, reaching 29--33~FPS when fully embedded. The core contribution
is an existence proof that high accuracy and real-time inference can co-exist under
strict hardware constraints on aerial platforms. The limitation is that the work targets
aerial footage with horizontal bounding boxes and does not discuss class imbalance or the
disambiguation of static vehicles.

Alás et al.~\cite{Alas2026} extend the hardware-feasibility argument to urban-scale
camera networks, where the challenge is sustaining cross-camera vehicle re-identification
across dozens of heterogeneous CCTV nodes without GPU hardware at each node.
Their modular architecture combines lightweight detection, transformer-based appearance
embeddings, and cross-camera association constrained by road topology and travel time.
Deployed across 27 real cameras using only CPU resources, the system maintains over 80\%
correct identity matches at 20--29~FPS per stream. The contribution is a direct
demonstration that scalable, affordable deployment of vehicle tracking is architecturally
feasible. The central limitation is that the system is designed for cross-camera
re-identification, a task structurally different from the per-frame oriented detection
required in our context, and it neither handles class imbalance nor filters stationary
vehicles.

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Detection of Minority Vehicle Types}

A recurring and underappreciated challenge in vehicle datasets is the severe imbalance
between common classes (passenger cars) and rare but safety-critical ones (motorcycles,
minibuses, articulated trucks). Espinosa et al.~\cite{Espinosa2020} address this gap
with a structured review of motorcycle detection and tracking in video, a class that
appears infrequently in most benchmarks yet represents one of the most vulnerable road
users. Their survey consolidates available algorithms and datasets, introducing the Urban
Motorbike Dataset (UMD) as a dedicated benchmark. The analysis identifies the
best-performing detectors on UMD and documents the field's current performance
boundaries. The contribution is precisely mapping the structural challenges of
small-minority-class detection, a situation directly analogous to the mototaxi and
minibus classes in the MTC challenge. The limitation is that the review neither evaluates
nor proposes oriented bounding box architectures, and stationary-vehicle filtering is
outside its scope.

Mo and Yan~\cite{Mo2020} confront the same imbalance problem from an experimental
angle, targeting small-vehicle detection in aerial images where class distributions
are highly skewed and pooling operations systematically degrade fine-grained features.
Their objective is an oriented vehicle detection framework built on Faster~R-CNN that
simultaneously addresses feature loss for small objects and category frequency imbalance.
The methodology combines an oversampling-and-stitching data augmentation scheme to
balance the training distribution, bilinear interpolation to amplify feature maps before
classification, and a center loss term in the multi-task objective that forces intra-class
feature representations to cluster, improving discrimination between visually similar
vehicle types. On the VEDAI dataset, the framework achieves 60.1\% mAP for oriented
bounding boxes, representing an 8\% gain over baseline Faster~R-CNN. The contribution is a
concrete recipe for combining data-level resampling with representation-level loss design
in oriented vehicle detection. The main deficiency is the two-stage architecture's
inference speed, which precludes real-time application, together with a tendency to
misclassify complex backgrounds as vehicles.

\subsection{Oriented Detection and Tracking from Aerial Platforms}

With the establishment of oriented bounding boxes as a superior representation for aerial
vehicle detection, recent work has focused on two complementary frontiers: measuring
whether OBB detectors are \emph{geometrically} accurate enough for downstream safety
metrics, and extending OBB detection to the more challenging problem of multi-object
tracking from a moving camera.

Ahmed et al.~\cite{Ahmed2025} address the first frontier by asking whether the dimensional imprecision of standard bounding box detectors distorts safety-critical metrics such as Time-to-Collision (TTC) when computed from UAV footage. Their study evaluates three YOLOv8 configurations (standard, YOLOv8+SAM (Segment Anything Model), and YOLOv8-OBB) by integrating each with a SIFT-based georeferencing pipeline and measuring vehicle dimensions against ground truth at drone altitudes of 60 and 90 metres. YOLOv8-OBB achieves the lowest mean RMSE of 0.99~m (with a 95\% CI of [0.80, 1.20]), compared to 1.32~m for YOLOv8+SAM and 3.31~m for standard YOLOv8, while running nearly in real time. The contribution is a compelling empirical argument that OBB detectors are not merely more precise geometrically, but that this precision translates to measurably better safety metrics. The key limitation is that no fine-tuning is performed on any local dataset (all three models are deployed off-the-shelf) and performance is sensitive to weather and illumination conditions.

Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2025} tackle the second frontier with ArbiTrack, a multi-object
tracking framework designed specifically for detecting and tracking arbitrarily oriented
vehicles from a \emph{moving} aerial vehicle. The core problem is the combined
interference of irregular camera motion, partial occlusions, and arbitrary target
orientations, all of which destabilize conventional tracking pipelines. ArbiTrack
integrates an OBB detector with three purpose-built modules: Multi-Scale Context
Aggregation (MCA) to preserve small-object features, Adaptive Motion Switching (AMS) to
compensate for non-linear drone maneuvers by switching dynamically between Kalman and
Unscented Kalman filters, and a Spatiotemporal Evolutionary Memory (SEM) module using
ConvGRU to maintain identity continuity under occlusion. On the authors' OriDrone dataset,
ArbiTrack achieves 44.6\% MOTA and 56.9\% IDF1, outperforming FairMOT by 6.7\% and
11.7\% respectively on those metrics; on the public UAVDT benchmark, results reach 47.6\%
MOTA and 67.4\% IDF1 with superior identity consistency (786 ID switches versus 1564 for
FairMOT). The contribution is a complete end-to-end framework that proves temporal memory
and motion-adaptive filtering are necessary for reliable OBB tracking from moving cameras.
The limitation is that the framework assumes every detected vehicle must be tracked
regardless of motion state --- a problem structurally different from the MTC setting,
where only moving vehicles are annotated --- and performance degrades under low-light
conditions.

The architectural frontier of single-stage OBB detectors is defined by Jocher
et al.~\cite{Jocher2026}, who address two persistent bottlenecks in the YOLO family:
the inference-time latency introduced by Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS), and the
computational overhead of Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) in the detection head.
YOLO26 resolves both with a dual-head design: a one-to-many branch for rich supervision
during training and a one-to-one branch for native end-to-end, NMS-free inference.
The training pipeline couples three complementary advances --- MuSGD, a hybrid Muon--SGD
optimizer adapted from large-language-model research; Progressive Loss, which gradually
shifts supervision weight toward the inference-time branch; and STAL (Small-Target-Aware
Label Assignment), which guarantees positive anchor coverage for tiny objects. On the
COCO benchmark, YOLO26 spans 40.9--57.5\% mAP at 1.7--11.8~ms latency on an NVIDIA T4
(TensorRT), with STAL specifically lifting small-object AP from 29.0 to 29.6. For OBB
tasks, YOLO26 also introduces a revised angle parameterisation that eliminates the $90^\circ$
rotational ambiguity inherent in nearly-square bounding boxes. The contribution is the
most recent and complete unification of NMS-free end-to-end detection with OBB-specific
training improvements. The limitation is that, as a very recent preprint, YOLO26 has not
yet been evaluated in domain-specific vehicle detection settings, leaving open how much of
its COCO advantage transfers to fine-grained, class-imbalanced urban traffic scenarios.

\subsection{Motion-State Inference and Stationary Vehicle Filtering}

A less explored but architecturally important thread in vehicle analysis is the inference
of motion state from video sequences, a capability that becomes essential when a dataset
annotates only moving vehicles, as is the case in our setting. Fernández-Rodríguez
et al.~\cite{FernandezRodriguez2023} address the problem of automatically detecting
vehicles with anomalous trajectories --- such as wrong-way drivers --- using only fixed
traffic cameras and without explicit motion labels. Their model compares per-vehicle
velocity vectors against those of spatially neighbouring trajectories, flagging outliers
as anomalous. The methodology chains per-frame YOLO detection with trajectory tracking,
velocity-vector estimation, and a spatial-neighbour comparison step. The system
successfully identifies wrong-way and erratic trajectories in real footage, demonstrating
that motion state can be reliably inferred from temporal patterns across frames rather than
from appearance alone. The contribution is precisely establishing that trajectory-level
reasoning --- not a single-frame detector --- is the appropriate tool for motion-state
discrimination. The critical limitation, however, is that the method is designed for
anomalous-versus-normal trajectory classification and does not explicitly separate fully
stationary (parked) vehicles from slowly circulating ones, which is the distinction that
matters most in our dataset.

\subsection*{Summary and Research Gap}

A clear pattern emerges across the reviewed literature: each study addresses one
dimension of the problem in isolation. Real-time baselines~\cite{Gomaa2022, Kumar2023, BinZuraimi2021}
establish solid detection and tracking pipelines, but rely on horizontal bounding boxes
and ignore class imbalance. Edge-deployment
works~\cite{Balamuralidhar2021, Alas2026} prove hardware feasibility yet do not tackle
orientation or skewed class distributions. Class-imbalance
research~\cite{Espinosa2020, Mo2020} demonstrates effective resampling and loss-engineering
strategies, but in two-stage architectures unsuitable for real-time inference.
Aerial OBB works~\cite{Ahmed2025, Chen2025, Jocher2026} push the geometric and
architectural frontier, yet none evaluates the impact of training on a dataset where
\emph{only moving vehicles are annotated}, nor do they quantify how much of the measured
metric error stems from that annotation bias rather than from detector limitations.
Motion-state inference~\cite{FernandezRodriguez2023} confirms that frame sequences are
necessary to separate moving from stationary vehicles, but does not connect this finding
to an OBB detection evaluation pipeline. No reviewed work addresses all of these dimensions
jointly: OBB detection, class-imbalance mitigation, and stationary-vehicle filtering
within a single, real-time-capable pipeline evaluated on urban intersection footage.
This multi-dimensional gap directly motivates the layered experimental design of the
present study.
