\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

The closest references come from several areas rather than a single research line: traffic surveillance, oriented detection, class-imbalance handling, and synthetic-data generation. Traffic-perception surveys describe the field conditions that also appear in the Lima clips: lighting variation, occlusion, congestion, camera placement, annotation cost, and heterogeneous vehicle fleets~\cite{Zhou2025TrafficSurveillanceSurvey,Azfar2024ComplexTrafficReview}. YOLO-based traffic systems explain why single-stage detectors remain attractive. Gomaa et al.~\cite{Gomaa2022} combine intermittent YOLOv2 detections with KLT optical flow for counting, Kumar et al.~\cite{Kumar2023} pair YOLOv5 with DeepSORT for real-time traffic management, and Bin Zuraimi and Kamaru Zaman~\cite{BinZuraimi2021} compare YOLOv3/v4 variants inside a detection-tracking pipeline. Their emphasis is speed and operational use; our ablation instead asks how a long-tailed traffic dataset should be augmented and scored when the labels are oriented boxes.

Work on deployment adds a complementary constraint: a traffic detector must be useful under real hardware and camera-network limitations. Balamuralidhar et al.~\cite{Balamuralidhar2021} present a UAV monitoring system that shares visual features across detection, tracking, speed estimation, and segmentation on embedded hardware. Alas et al.~\cite{Alas2026} consider multi-camera urban tracking, where association across CCTV streams must scale without assigning a high-end GPU to every camera. This line of work is important for municipal use cases, but it addresses tracking, counting, or re-identification more directly than the per-frame rotated-box augmentation problem considered here.

Another relevant thread is domain generalization across cameras. Koga et al.~\cite{Koga2021VehicleDomainAdaptation} study vehicle detector adaptation between different source and target domains using adversarial prediction alignment. Vora et al.~\cite{Vora2023GeneralizationMultiview} examine multi-view perception under camera-placement and scene variation. Although these studies do not target Lima traffic imagery, they motivate the same methodological concern: if all training evidence comes from a small set of observed frames, a detector may learn background or viewpoint regularities. In this setting, augmentation helps only if it increases diversity without corrupting the annotation geometry.

The use of OBBs responds to geometric limitations of horizontal boxes. Remote-sensing benchmarks and surveys have emphasized this point for dense scenes, arbitrary headings, and small targets~\cite{Ding2022DOTA,Wang2025OBBSurvey,Zand2022OBB}. Several detector designs then try to make the model itself orientation-aware: Oriented R-CNN aligns proposals with rotated objects~\cite{Xie2021OrientedRCNN}, ReDet introduces rotation-equivariant representation learning~\cite{Han2021ReDet}, and S2ANet aligns features for oriented detection~\cite{Han2022S2ANet}. Loss formulations based on Gaussian or Mahalanobis distances further illustrate why angular error is not a minor detail in high-precision rotated localization~\cite{Yang2021KLD,Wen2022MahalanobisRotatedDetection}. For vehicle analysis, Ahmed et al.~\cite{Ahmed2025} show that OBB and segmentation-assisted representations can reduce measurement error relative to axis-aligned boxes.

The YOLO family remains relevant because traffic applications need a practical compromise between accuracy, latency, and deployment friction. YOLOv10 strengthens the case for end-to-end single-stage detection by reducing reliance on conventional post-processing~\cite{Wang2024YOLOv10}. The experiments use Ultralytics YOLO26s-OBB, which places oriented detection in the same real-time family as detection, segmentation, pose, and classification~\cite{Jocher2026}. OBB-specific work such as task-wise sampling convolutions also shows that classification and localization features can become inconsistent if orientation is not handled carefully~\cite{Huang2025TSConv}. For the present dataset, the unresolved issue is not the availability of OBB detectors; it is whether the training data can be modified without weakening the rotated labels.

Long-tailed class frequencies and small object scale are particularly important in heterogeneous traffic. Espinosa et al.~\cite{Espinosa2020} review motorcycle detection and highlight the need for focused treatment of vulnerable or minority road-user categories. Mo and Yan~\cite{Mo2020} address imbalance in oriented aerial vehicle detection through oversampling, feature amplification, and center loss. More broadly, small-object surveys explain why compact targets can disappear in deep feature hierarchies or become ambiguous in dense context~\cite{Cheng2023SmallObjectSurvey,Nikouei2025SmallObjectSurvey}. These studies justify data-level balancing, but they do not define a train-only procedure for adding rare traffic vehicles while preserving OBB labels.

Synthetic augmentation is useful only when it improves the downstream detector, not merely when the generated image looks plausible. Weber et al.~\cite{Weber2021ArtificialImagesVehicleDetection} report gains from artificial aerial vehicle images in low-data regimes when they are combined with real samples. Mumuni et al.~\cite{Mumuni2024} review augmentation families and note that real-plus-synthetic training can work when the added examples support the task. Diffusion-based surveys and controllable augmentation methods add semantic flexibility, but also raise concerns about alignment, bias, cost, and quality assessment~\cite{Alimisis2025Advances,Benkedadra2024,ZhuH2025}. Those concerns are sharper for OBB detection: a pasted or generated vehicle can look acceptable and still be unusable if the rotated boundary no longer follows the pixels.

Segmentation-assisted composition is a middle ground between full-frame generation and ordinary geometric augmentation. Rahat et al.~\cite{Rahat2024} combine off-the-shelf segmentation with generative background diversification to preserve foreground subjects. Huang et al.~\cite{HuangW2025} analyze object-centric synthetic compositions and warn that simple copy-paste can introduce edge artifacts or context mismatch. The production path is intentionally conservative: it extracts real vehicles with SAM masks, places them only in train-split slots, leaves pixels outside the mask unchanged, and writes OBB labels from the target geometry instead of estimating them after rendering.

Table~\ref{tab:related_work_summary} summarizes how these lines relate to the present experiment. Prior work motivates the components, but it does not directly answer the question tested here: whether a train-only SAM copy-paste delta helps a long-tailed Peruvian traffic OBB dataset when the validation data, class identifiers, motion filter, and rotated geometry are kept fixed.
