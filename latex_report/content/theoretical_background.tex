\section{Background}
\label{sec:theoretical_background}

\subsection{Traffic Video Detection}
\label{sec:traffic_video_detection}

Intelligent transport systems use visual sensing to estimate vehicle flows, classify vehicle types, and support traffic management decisions. Camera-based monitoring is attractive because existing infrastructure can capture dense spatial information without additional physical sensors. However, urban video is highly variable: illumination changes, occlusion, congestion, camera placement, and heterogeneous fleets alter vehicle appearance substantially~\cite{Zhou2025TrafficSurveillanceSurvey,Azfar2024ComplexTrafficReview}. Modern object detection surveys also show that performance depends not only on the detector architecture, but also on the distribution and quality of the data used for training~\cite{Zou2023ObjectDetectionSurvey}. These conditions motivate treating dataset preparation, annotation geometry, and evaluation design as core parts of the detection problem.

\subsection{Oriented Bounding Boxes}
\label{sec:oriented_bounding_boxes}

Object detectors commonly localize instances with horizontal bounding boxes (HBBs), but HBBs are inefficient for vehicles observed at arbitrary headings. They include unnecessary background and can increase overlap between nearby objects. Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs) add rotation to the localization target, either through center-size-angle parameters or four rotated corners, so the box better matches the vehicle footprint~\cite{Wang2025OBBSurvey,Zand2022OBB}. This makes OBBs more suitable for elevated traffic viewpoints, where orientation matters and geometric error has practical consequences~\cite{Ahmed2025}.

\subsection{Rotated Intersection over Union}
\label{sec:riou_metric}

Rotated Intersection over Union (rIoU) measures the geometric agreement between two oriented boxes after converting them into polygons. Unlike conventional IoU, it evaluates overlap on the rotated quadrilaterals themselves, so it is sensitive to angular misalignment as well as center and scale error. This is especially relevant for elongated vehicles, where small heading differences can reduce overlap sharply even when the predicted center is close to the target. In this work, every OBB is validated, converted to a four-vertex polygon, and evaluated with OpenCV polygon operations. For a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$, rIoU is defined as
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{rIoU}(B_p,B_g) =
  \frac{\left|P(B_p)\cap P(B_g)\right|}
       {\left|P(B_p)\cup P(B_g)\right|},
  \label{eq:riou}
\end{equation}
where $P(\cdot)$ denotes the polygon induced by an OBB and $|\cdot|$ denotes polygon area. A value of 1 indicates perfect overlap and 0 indicates no overlap. Values near a threshold, such as 0.50 or 0.80, are accepted as matches only when the rotated geometry satisfies that threshold, which makes rIoU stricter than horizontal IoU for diagonal or thin objects.

\subsection{Macro AP-rIoU}
\label{sec:macro_ap_riou}

The evaluation protocol implements Macro AP-rIoU over the nine vehicle classes and seven rIoU thresholds, $\mathcal{T}=\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.80\}$. For each class and threshold, predictions are sorted by confidence, matched greedily to unused ground-truth boxes from the same frame and class, and summarized with 101-point interpolated Average Precision (AP). The final score gives equal weight to every class:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \operatorname{MacroAP}_{rIoU}
  = \frac{1}{9}\sum_{c=1}^{9}
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}
    \operatorname{AP}_{c,\tau}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:macro_ap_riou}
\end{equation}
Macro averaging is important in a long-tailed dataset because strong performance on cars cannot compensate for weak performance on rare categories. The metric therefore aligns with the study's focus on minority-class augmentation.

\subsection{Small Objects and Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:small_objects_imbalance}

Traffic scenes from elevated cameras contain many compact or distant vehicles that occupy few pixels. Small-object surveys identify downsampling, weak feature support, and dense context as common detector failure modes~\cite{Cheng2023SmallObjectSurvey,Nikouei2025SmallObjectSurvey}. Class imbalance compounds the problem because frequent classes dominate the training signal, while rare categories receive fewer examples and less variation during optimization. Work on motorcycle detection and oriented aerial vehicle detection shows that vulnerable or minority vehicle classes often require dedicated modeling or data-balancing strategies~\cite{Espinosa2020,Mo2020}. These observations justify both the class-aware metric and the data-level augmentation strategy used in this study.

\subsection{Segmentation-Assisted Synthetic Augmentation}
\label{sec:segmentation_assisted_augmentation_background}

Synthetic data can improve detector training when it increases diversity without breaking label validity. Prior studies show that combining real and synthetic images can be useful, but they also warn about domain gaps and unrealistic artifacts~\cite{Weber2021ArtificialImagesVehicleDetection,Mumuni2024}. Recent augmentation methods therefore use segmentation or regional control to preserve object identity and spatial consistency~\cite{Rahat2024,Benkedadra2024,ZhuH2025}. The method documented here follows that conservative direction by using segmentation masks and copy-paste composition instead of full-frame generation in the final training set.
