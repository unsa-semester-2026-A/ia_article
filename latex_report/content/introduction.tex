\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Camera-based traffic surveillance is an important component of modern intelligent transport systems because it can estimate vehicle flow, support incident analysis, and reduce dependence on intrusive road sensors. Deep learning has improved this task by learning visual representations directly from images~\cite{Zou2023ObjectDetectionSurvey}. Nevertheless, real urban traffic video remains difficult because camera height, viewpoint, shadows, occlusion, congestion, and vehicle diversity can change the appearance of the same class across locations~\cite{Zhou2025TrafficSurveillanceSurvey,Azfar2024ComplexTrafficReview}. These conditions are not only technical nuisances; they affect whether a detector can support reliable traffic analytics in operational environments.

These difficulties are especially relevant for Lima, Peru, where fixed surveillance cameras observe dense and heterogeneous traffic. A detector trained only on observed frames from a limited set of cameras may learn scene-specific background patterns rather than robust vehicle representations. It may also underrepresent rare classes and distant vehicles, which are precisely the cases that tend to be most fragile under dense traffic conditions. Domain adaptation and multi-view studies show that detector performance can change when source and target cameras differ in scene layout, viewpoint, illumination, or object scale~\cite{Koga2021VehicleDomainAdaptation,Vora2023GeneralizationMultiview}. This creates a practical gap between available camera infrastructure and dependable vehicle-level perception.

Vehicle localization also has a geometric component. Horizontal bounding boxes are simple, but they include unnecessary background when vehicles appear at diagonal orientations. Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs) better match the vehicle footprint and are widely used for objects observed at arbitrary angles~\cite{Wang2025OBBSurvey,Zand2022OBB}. This representation is useful for traffic scenes with turning vehicles, dense lanes, and elevated camera viewpoints. It also makes evaluation more sensitive to angle and shape errors, because rotated intersection over union penalizes misaligned boxes more directly than horizontal IoU~\cite{Ahmed2025}.

Class imbalance further limits detector reliability. Urban fleets include frequent categories, such as cars, and less frequent categories, such as buses, mototaxis, and motorcycles. Rare and compact objects receive less training evidence and often occupy fewer pixels, which weakens the feature support available to modern detectors. Prior work on small-object detection and motorcycle detection highlights the difficulty of learning these categories from imbalanced visual data~\cite{Espinosa2020,Cheng2023SmallObjectSurvey,Nikouei2025SmallObjectSurvey}. In OBB detection, this problem is amplified by angular sensitivity, crowding, and the strict matching required by rotated metrics.

Data augmentation is a practical response to limited diversity, but it must preserve labels. Full-frame synthetic generation can introduce visual artifacts, shift object boundaries, or create geometric inconsistencies between pixels and annotations. This is risky for rotated-box evaluation, where small localization errors affect the score and can distort class-wise conclusions. Segmentation-assisted copy-paste offers a more controlled alternative because it can reuse real vehicle pixels, isolate objects with masks, and insert them into selected training backgrounds while preserving explicit OBB annotations~\cite{Rahat2024,Mumuni2024,HuangW2025}. The central research problem addressed in this paper is therefore how to increase minority-class evidence for oriented vehicle detection without contaminating validation data or weakening annotation fidelity.

This paper proposes a segmentation-assisted augmentation workflow for oriented vehicle detection in Peruvian traffic imagery. The method characterizes the source data, converts the annotations into YOLO-OBB format, extracts vehicle masks with the Segment Anything Model (SAM), generates new training samples through copy-paste insertion, validates the resulting labels, and assesses predictions with Macro AP-rIoU. Augmented samples are added only to the training set, while the validation split remains unchanged. IC-Light relighting was explored during preliminary experimentation, but it is not part of the final production workflow because the implemented contribution prioritizes deterministic label preservation over unverified photorealistic synthesis.

The objective of this study is to design and document a reproducible augmentation methodology that addresses class imbalance while preserving oriented annotation geometry and validation integrity. The contribution is a complete data-centric pipeline that includes dataset auditing, OBB conversion, train-only SAM-assisted copy-paste generation, validated YOLO-OBB label writing, consistent YOLO26s-OBB training conditions, Macro AP-rIoU evaluation code, and final evaluation packaging. This scope is significant because it provides a controlled basis for future detector comparisons in heterogeneous urban traffic scenes without claiming unsupported accuracy gains.
