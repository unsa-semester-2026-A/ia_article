\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Traffic cameras are attractive for urban mobility analysis because they can observe vehicle flow without adding physical sensors to the road network. Modern detectors have made this setting more practical by learning visual features directly from image data~\cite{Zou2023ObjectDetectionSurvey}. In real surveillance feeds, however, the same vehicle category may look different across cameras because of viewpoint, mounting height, shadows, occlusion, congestion, and local fleet composition~\cite{Zhou2025TrafficSurveillanceSurvey,Azfar2024ComplexTrafficReview}. These factors matter beyond model accuracy benchmarks: they influence whether visual detections can be trusted as inputs for operational traffic analysis.

The Peruvian traffic setting considered here has the same difficulty in a concrete form. Fixed cameras in Lima record dense, mixed traffic, but the available frames represent only a limited set of viewpoints and visual conditions. A detector trained on those frames can overfit to camera-specific background cues, and minority categories or distant vehicles may receive too little visual evidence during training. Prior domain-adaptation and multi-view studies show that changes in scene layout, viewpoint, illumination, and object scale can alter detector behavior across camera domains~\cite{Koga2021VehicleDomainAdaptation,Vora2023GeneralizationMultiview}. The practical problem is therefore not only to train a detector, but to prepare training data that encourages generalizable vehicle evidence without leaking information into validation.

The localization target adds another constraint. Horizontal boxes are easy to store and evaluate, but they become loose when cars, buses, or motorcycles appear diagonally in the image. Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs) follow the visible footprint more closely and are commonly used when objects can appear at arbitrary angles~\cite{Wang2025OBBSurvey,Zand2022OBB}. This representation is well matched to elevated traffic cameras, turning movements, and packed lanes, but it also makes the evaluation stricter: rotated overlap penalizes heading and footprint errors that a horizontal box may hide~\cite{Ahmed2025}.

Class imbalance makes this geometry problem harder. Cars dominate many urban traffic datasets, while buses, mototaxis, motorcycles, and other less frequent classes appear with fewer examples and often at smaller image scales. Small-object and motorcycle-detection studies describe how limited pixel support, crowded context, and uneven class frequencies weaken recognition for these categories~\cite{Espinosa2020,Cheng2023SmallObjectSurvey,Nikouei2025SmallObjectSurvey}. In an OBB task, the same weakness affects both classification and rotated localization, so rare-class errors can have a large effect on class-balanced evaluation.

Augmentation can address limited diversity, but only if the new samples remain geometrically trustworthy. Full-image synthesis may add visual variety, yet it can also create artifacts, move boundaries, or weaken the correspondence between pixels and rotated labels. Those errors are especially costly when evaluation depends on polygon overlap. Segmentation-assisted copy-paste is a narrower alternative: it reuses real foreground vehicles, isolates them with masks, and places them in selected training backgrounds while keeping explicit OBB annotations~\cite{Rahat2024,Mumuni2024,HuangW2025}. The research question of this paper is how to increase minority-class evidence for oriented vehicle detection while preserving validation integrity and label fidelity.

To address this question, the study builds and evaluates a mask-guided augmentation workflow for Peruvian traffic imagery. The pipeline audits the dataset, converts annotations to YOLO-OBB format, extracts vehicle masks with the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{Kirillov2023SAM}, generates copy-paste training samples, validates the written labels, and evaluates predictions with Macro AP-rIoU. Synthetic samples are introduced only in training. The validation frames remain raw and unchanged. IC-Light relighting~\cite{Zhang2025ICLight} was tested during exploration, but it is excluded from the final production data because the implemented contribution favors deterministic label preservation over unverified photorealistic generation.

The objective is to document a reproducible methodology and test it through an ablation study. The contribution includes dataset auditing, OBB conversion, train-only SAM-assisted copy-paste generation, YOLO-OBB label validation, consistent YOLO26s-OBB training conditions, Macro AP-rIoU evaluation code, final evaluation packaging, and a condition-by-condition analysis of the uploaded artifacts. The current evidence identifies the raw-data baseline as the strongest available final result; it does not verify a final-metric gain from the synthetic-data conditions.
