\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Camera-based traffic surveillance is an important component of modern intelligent transport systems. It can estimate vehicle flow, support incident analysis, and reduce dependence on intrusive road sensors. Deep learning has improved this task by learning visual representations directly from images~\cite{Zou2023ObjectDetectionSurvey}. However, urban traffic video remains difficult. Camera height, viewpoint, shadows, occlusion, congestion, and vehicle diversity can change the appearance of the same class across locations~\cite{Zhou2025TrafficSurveillanceSurvey,Azfar2024ComplexTrafficReview}.

These difficulties are relevant for Lima, Peru, where fixed cameras observe dense and heterogeneous traffic. A detector trained only on the available observed frames may learn camera-specific background patterns. It may also underrepresent rare categories and distant vehicles. Domain adaptation and multi-view studies show that detector performance can change when source and target cameras differ in scene layout, viewpoint, or lighting~\cite{Koga2021VehicleDomainAdaptation,Vora2023GeneralizationMultiview}. This creates a practical gap between available surveillance infrastructure and reliable vehicle-level traffic analytics.

Vehicle localization also has a geometric component. Horizontal bounding boxes are simple, but they include unnecessary background when vehicles appear at diagonal orientations. Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs) better match the vehicle footprint and are widely used for objects observed at arbitrary angles~\cite{Wang2025OBBSurvey,Zand2022OBB}. This representation is useful for traffic scenes with turning vehicles, dense lanes, and elevated camera viewpoints. It also makes evaluation more sensitive to angle and shape errors, because rotated intersection over union penalizes misaligned boxes more directly than horizontal IoU~\cite{Ahmed2025}.

Class imbalance further limits detector reliability. Urban fleets include frequent classes, such as cars, and less frequent categories, such as buses, mototaxis, and motorcycles. Rare and compact objects receive less training evidence and often occupy fewer pixels. Prior work on small-object detection and motorcycle detection highlights the difficulty of learning these categories from imbalanced visual data~\cite{Espinosa2020,Cheng2023SmallObjectSurvey,Nikouei2025SmallObjectSurvey}. In OBB detection, this problem is amplified by angular sensitivity and crowded scenes.

Data augmentation is one way to increase training diversity, but it must preserve labels. Full-frame synthetic generation can introduce visual artifacts or shift object boundaries. This is risky for rotated-box evaluation, where small geometric errors affect the score. Segmentation-assisted copy-paste offers a more controlled alternative. It can reuse real vehicle pixels, isolate objects with masks, and insert them into selected training backgrounds while preserving explicit OBB annotations~\cite{Rahat2024,Mumuni2024,HuangW2025}.

The problem addressed in this paper is the limited representation of rare, small, distant, and differently oriented vehicles in urban traffic imagery. This limitation can reduce detector reliability in dense and heterogeneous scenes. Class imbalance, occlusion, object scale, and angular variation further affect oriented vehicle detection. Conventional synthetic generation may also introduce visual artifacts or geometric inconsistencies. These errors can reduce OBB label accuracy and compromise evaluation validity.

This paper proposes a segmentation-assisted augmentation workflow for oriented vehicle detection. Its main objective is to increase training diversity while preserving annotation geometry and validation integrity. The method first characterizes the traffic dataset. It then converts the annotations into YOLO-OBB format. Vehicle masks are extracted with the Segment Anything Model (SAM). New training samples are generated through copy-paste. The resulting labels are validated, and performance is assessed with Macro AP-rIoU. Augmented samples are added only to the training set. The original validation split remains unchanged. IC-Light is not used in the final workflow. Relighting experiments are treated only as preliminary tests.

This investigation addresses class imbalance while preserving the integrity of the validation data. It avoids full-frame generative synthesis by reusing real vehicle pixels and maintaining explicit OBB annotations. This approach reduces the risk of label corruption, unrealistic geometry, and evaluation bias. Its main contribution is a train-only SAM-assisted copy-paste dataset with validated YOLO-OBB labels and consistent training and evaluation conditions. The proposed workflow also provides a transparent basis for future comparisons of oriented vehicle detectors in heterogeneous traffic scenes.
